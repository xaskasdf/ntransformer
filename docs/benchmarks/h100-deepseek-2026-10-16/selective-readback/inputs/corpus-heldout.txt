The village stood between a long lake and a line of low hills. Every morning, Mara walked down the stone steps to open the workshop that her grandfather had built. The door stuck in wet weather, the windows faced east, and a wooden clock above the bench usually ran four minutes slow. She had learned to repair chairs and tables there, but the job waiting for her that autumn was different: the village school needed a cabinet for its collection of maps.

The teacher arrived carrying a roll of paper tied with blue string. There were maps of the valley, sketches of old roads, and a drawing of the lake made before the northern bridge existed. Some sheets were wider than the workshop table. Others had torn corners or handwritten notes in faded ink. Mara measured the largest sheet twice, then drew a shallow drawer on a scrap of cardboard. The cabinet would need six drawers, each wide enough for a map to lie flat, and legs tall enough to keep the paper away from a damp floor.

Finding suitable wood took three days. The merchant had narrow boards that would work for the sides, but nothing broad enough for the top. A neighbor offered an old dining table stored in a barn. Its surface was scratched and one leg was missing, yet the wood beneath the dust was sound. Mara paid for it with a promise to mend two kitchen stools before winter. She carried the table home on a cart, removed the rusty screws, and left the boards in the workshop to dry.

On Thursday the wind changed. Rain swept across the lake and pushed fallen leaves into the drain beside the school. The teacher came back to ask whether the cabinet could be finished sooner. Water had entered the room where the maps were stored, and the children had moved the collection onto their desks. Mara explained that glue needed time to set, even when a deadline was urgent. She could build a temporary raised shelf that afternoon, but rushing the drawers would leave them crooked.

They chose the shelf. Two older pupils helped carry it to the school while the younger children sorted the maps by size. One girl found a small drawing of an orchard where the football field now stood. A boy noticed that the old road crossed the river at a place with no bridge. Their teacher asked them to write down their questions instead of guessing at the answers. By the end of the day, the class had filled a page with things they wanted to ask the oldest residents of the village.

Work on the cabinet continued during the following week. Mara cut the drawer sides in pairs so that matching pieces would have the same length. She checked each corner with a square and marked the hidden surfaces with a soft pencil. The top required two joined boards, which she clamped overnight. While the glue dried, she repaired the neighbor's stools and replaced a broken handle on the workshop door. Small repairs kept the room useful while the larger project occupied the main bench.

The first drawer slid smoothly, but the second caught near the back. At first Mara suspected that a side had warped. She removed it, measured the opening, and discovered a thin curl of wood trapped beneath the runner. Once she cleaned the groove, the drawer moved without resistance. She kept the shaving on the bench for a moment, amused that such a small piece could make the entire drawer seem badly built. Then she swept the floor and checked the remaining runners before fitting the other four drawers.

The school arranged a small exhibition for the cabinet's arrival. Families brought photographs, letters, and stories about places shown on the maps. The retired ferryman remembered the orchard and knew why the old road ended at the river: travelers had once crossed by boat. Another resident explained that the northern bridge had been rebuilt after a flood, which accounted for two different dates written on the same drawing. The pupils added these details to their notes, carefully recording who had told each story.

Mara delivered the cabinet on a clear Saturday. Four people carried it up the school steps while she guided the front legs past the narrow doorway. The teacher opened every drawer, placed a clean sheet of paper inside, and began arranging the maps. The children wanted to put their own sketches in the lowest drawer. There was room, she said, provided that each drawing included a name, a date, and a description of the place it showed.

When Mara returned to the workshop, the afternoon light had moved across the bench. The room felt unusually empty without the cabinet. She put away the clamps, sharpened a plane, and finally adjusted the clock above the door. Outside, the lake was calm enough to reflect the hills. She wrote the next week's repairs in her notebook, closed the windows, and walked home carrying the teacher's thank-you letter in her coat pocket.
